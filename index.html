Incident Commander Role by Phase

Your responsibilities move through three distinct phases. Preparation builds the foundation, execution tests your command decisions, and evaluation turns the exercise into real improvement.

01 · Before

Preparation

  • Review the AEP — confirm command structure, authorities, and agency roles.
  • Understand scenario & objectives — know the emergency type and what is being tested.
  • Confirm your role — ensure official IC designation and ICP-to-EOC reporting lines.
  • Join planning meetings — help design the exercise around the systems you rely on.
02 · During

Execution

  • Establish command & the ICP — set up a clearly identifiable command location.
  • Conduct size-up — gather information fast, prioritize, task resources.
  • Build & communicate the IAP — state objectives, strategy, critical functions.
  • Assign command staff — Safety Officer, PIO, Liaison Officer.
  • Coordinate multi-agency response — fire, police, EMS, ops, tenants.
03 · After

Evaluation

  • Hot Wash — immediate feedback on what worked and what did not.
  • After Action Report — document strengths, gaps, corrective actions, timelines.
  • Update the AEP — fold lessons learned back into procedures.
💡
Command principle: the IC directs, but does not personally fight the fire. Establish the ICP early, delegate the Safety Officer, PIO and Liaison Officer, and keep your own span of control manageable.

Tabletop Exercise Team Labels

Two distinct sets of labels matter: the staff who run the drill, and the simulated response teams that play in it.

Exercise Staff — Who Runs the Drill

Players / Participants

Actively role-play their real-world positions — including you as IC — and respond to the scenario.

Facilitators

Present scenario updates (injects) and guide discussion. They do not participate in response decisions.

Evaluators / Observers

Subject matter experts who watch and document performance against objectives without intervening.

Note-takers / Scribes

Record the timeline, key decisions, and identified gaps for the After Action Report.

Controllers

Ensure safety and scenario progression as scripted. More common in full-scale exercises.

Simulated Response Teams — Functional Groups

🎯Incident / Unified Command

Core leadership: IC, Safety Officer, Liaison Officer, PIO.

🚒Fire / Rescue & ARFF

Aircraft Rescue and Firefighting plus structural fire response.

🚑EMS / Medical / Triage

Emergency medical services, casualty collection, hospital coordination.

🚔Law Enforcement / Security

Airport police, perimeter control, scene security.

🛫Airport Operations / Airside

Runway closures, NOTAMs, airfield management.

👨‍👩‍👧Passenger / Family Assistance

Uninjured passenger gathering, Friends & Relatives Center, reunification.

📢Public Information / Media

Press briefings and public messaging.

📦Logistics / Finance

Resource ordering, tracking, cost documentation.

🏢Airlines / Tenant Reps

Airline operations and ground handler coordination.

💡
How to choose labels: match them to your exercise objectives. Use ICS sections (Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Admin) to test internal command. Use department names for smaller single-organization drills. Always mirror the terminology in your AEP.

Key Locations — Airport Reference Layout

Generic airport layout for exercise planning: Runway oriented north–south, parallel taxiway and apron on the west side, passenger terminal beyond the apron, perimeter road along the east boundary, and the airport support zone to the south. Hover any numbered marker to highlight it.

💡
Placement logic: the runway runs north–south with the apron and terminal on the west side. ARFF sits southwest of the apron for rapid runway access. The ICP is positioned upwind (west of the crash site) and outside the warm zone. Triage and CCP are further west, using the apron and ARFF zone. The FAC is in the terminal complex, off the operational area. The morgue uses a remote corner of the airfield, well away from the terminal.

Aircraft Incident Zones

The scene is organized into three concentric safety zones, adapted from hazardous materials response. Aircraft accidents involve high fuel loads and composite materials, so the boundaries matter.

Hot Zone

Red / Exclusion Zone

The immediate hazard area: wreckage, active fire, fuel, structural collapse, and hazardous cargo.

Only fully PPE-equipped rescue personnel (e.g. ARFF) enter — and only for life-saving missions. The IC remains outside this zone.

Warm Zone

Yellow / Transition Zone

A controlled buffer between the hazard and the safe area. Hosts triage, casualty collection, decontamination, and PPE donning/doffing.

Access limited to authorized personnel. The Incident Command Post may sit here or just inside the Cold Zone.

Cold Zone

Green / Support Zone

The safe area outside the hazard, free from contamination. Contains the Command Post, staging areas, media briefing area, and the temporary morgue.

This is where the Incident Commander and support staff typically operate.

Perimeters

Inner Cordon

Controls access to the immediate hazard area — roughly the Hot / Warm boundary.

Outer Cordon

A larger secure perimeter for emergency vehicle movement and crowd control, encompassing the whole operation.

Types of Airport Emergencies

Categorizing potential emergencies helps you test specific response plans and resource coordination. Frameworks from ICAO, EASA and the FAA group them broadly like this.

✈️

Aircraft-Related

  • Accidents on or off the airport
  • In-flight emergencies — Full Emergency and Local Standby
  • Gear-up landing, disabled aircraft on runway
  • Ground collisions and taxiway incidents
🏢

Non-Aircraft

  • Structural fires — terminal, hangar, cargo
  • Fuel farm / storage fires
  • HazMat / Dangerous Goods spills
  • Radiological incidents
  • Utility failures (e.g. movement area lighting)
  • Water rescue situations (airport perimeter)
🔒

Security & Unlawful Interference

  • Sabotage and bomb threats
  • Unlawfully seized aircraft (hijack)
  • Armed attacks and hostage situations
  • Acts of sabotage at airport installations
🩺

Public Health & Environmental

  • Communicable disease spread via air travel
  • Severe outbreak affecting airport staff
  • Natural disasters — earthquake, flood, tornado
  • Volcanic ash events
⚡

Compound Emergencies

  • Two or more emergencies simultaneously or in quick succession
  • Example: aircraft accident + fuel fire + HazMat spill
  • Excellent stress test for your Incident Command System and resource management

Fuel & Airport Considerations

⛽
Check your airport's fuel storage and refuelling capability. Some smaller airports have no aviation fuel storage or refuelling facility on-site. Aircraft arriving must carry sufficient fuel for the return leg, or fuel must be pre-positioned by the operator.

Consider how your national supply chain works: where is jet fuel stored, how is it distributed to your airport, and what happens if that supply chain is disrupted during an emergency?
💡
Exercise injects this enables: "Aircraft declares minimum fuel and requests priority landing — no fuel available for turn-around." Or "Operator requests emergency fuel bowser delivery by road/sea." Both test your logistics and liaison functions hard.
